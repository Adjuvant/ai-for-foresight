# Strategic Foresight

Umbrella term for methods that reduce uncertainty about the future — horizon scanning, trend analysis, forecasting, scenarios, modelling and simulation — positioned by Kohler (2021) as an input that conditions policy rather than a substitute for planning, and as complementary to, not in conflict with, empirical data collection and resilience building.

## Key Claims
- Strategic foresight is an umbrella term covering qualitative and quantitative means for monitoring clues and indicators of evolving trends and developments, and is best and most useful when directly linked to the analysis of policy implications; foresight in government cannot define policy but can condition policies to be more appropriate, flexible and robust — 'not planning — merely a step in planning' (per Coates 1985). ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- The sources of understanding in all strategic foresight methods are ultimately combinations of expert intuition, causal reasoning, and data from the past, the present, and simulated futures. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Forecasts have an advantage over scenarios in that they are falsifiable, which enables their systematic evaluation even when results are disappointing. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- There is no inherent conflict between strategic foresight and approaches focused on collecting more empirical data or increasing resilience; foresight can help prioritize which areas would profit from more data collection, while more data and learning from disasters can improve foresight. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Systematic assessments of the success of qualitative methods such as scenario planning remain difficult, though there is evidence for measurable improvements in specific forecast areas such as weather forecasts and, to a more limited degree, generalist forecasts. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Educational investment in generalist foresight remains modest: futures literacy is rarely part of the curriculum even as an elective, despite demand for futures-related tasks in many professions. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Foresight should be brought closer to the sites of its application ('foresight-on-site'), going beyond the anticipation of future developments to consider the further impacts of science, research, policies and actions on society and humanity, with ethical, legal, cultural and value implications (per Saritas et al. 2022). ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])
- The qualitative social sciences — anthropology and sociology — can play a key role in creating new foresight by adding empirically grounded, theoretically informed layers of knowledge about how human experience, values and social practices will shape possible futures, layers that quantitative foresight alone cannot supply. ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])

## Open Questions

Kohler (2021) leaves open how qualitative methods (scenario planning, horizon scanning) can be systematically evaluated, where the boundary between foresight and risk management should sit, and whether futures literacy should become a standard part of education. The LLM-era foresight literature (deep-research agents, agentic signal intelligence) is now re-encountering these same questions.

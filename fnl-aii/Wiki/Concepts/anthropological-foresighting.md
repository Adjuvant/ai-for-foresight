# Anthropological Foresighting

The ethnographic/anthropological strand of qualitative foresight: methods that investigate how people are likely to be configured in possible futures through futures-focused ethnography, surveys, documentary and workshops; Pink et al. (2023) introduce design anthropological foresighting — futures anthropology, design anthropology, phenomenology and social practice theory in dialogue — producing non-predictive foresights and 'reframing concepts' that correct dominant sociotechnical imaginaries.

## Key Claims
- Ethnographic foresighting is not a predictive method: it identifies how people and things are likely to be configured in futures rather than exactly what people will do or directly experience in the future. ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])
- Foresight is non-deterministic — it has a multiple vision of futures and is open to opportunities rather than being predictive — and it encourages proactivity, the possibility and ability to appropriate the future away from the forecasts that predetermine its path (per Lindgren, Pink and Fors 2021). ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])
- Dominant industry and government future visions — sociotechnical imaginaries of the societal problems that AI and automated decision-making will solve — are implausible as stated, because people improvise, adapt and tailor technologies to everyday circumstances rather than being unidirectionally changed by them (per Jasanoff 2015). ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])
- The theoretical commitment of design anthropological foresighting — futures anthropology (per Salazar et al. 2017), design anthropology as a reframing approach (per Kjaersgaard and Otto 2012), phenomenological anthropology and social practice theory (per Shove et al. 2012) — is its differentiating feature relative to existing ethnographic foresighting methods. ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])
- Design anthropological foresighting produces two outcomes: specific foresights about the principles, priorities and values that will drive people's future experiences and practices, and reframing concepts — such as 'Set and Notify' replacing industry's 'Set and Forget', and 'Everyday Innovators' replacing 'Plug and Play' — that correct the framing of automation futures rather than predicting specific future situations. ([[Raw/pinkDesignAnthropologicalForesighting2023]])

## Methodological Repertoire
Pink et al. (2023) demonstrate the approach in the Digital Energy Futures project (ARC Linkage LP180100203, with Ausgrid, AusNet Services and Energy Consumers Australia): stage 1 industry report analysis of 64 technology/energy reports identifying 14 macrotrends; stage 2 ethnographic futures research with 72 Australian households (short-term ethnography, video tours, participant-produced materials, future-focused comic strips); stage 3 the annual Energy Consumers Behavioural Survey (4,661 national responses over two years) used to cross-check qualitative findings; stage 4 design ethnographic futures workshops (10 workshops, 42 participants, online via zoom/miroboard) using map-based activities and 'thing ethnography' (per Giaccardi et al. 2016) in which participants enacted future air technologies; plus an ethnographic documentary and design ethnographic futures scenarios.

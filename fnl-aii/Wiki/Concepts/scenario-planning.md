# Scenario Planning

What-if exploration of possible or preferable futures without quantifying their likelihood — creativity techniques (morphological boxes, futures wheel), the classic Shell 2x2 matrix with wind tunnelling, backcasting, role play, wargames and fiction; Kohler (2021) reviews the method family and argues CBRN contexts should favour closed, invitation-only formats over participatory ones because of information hazards.

## Key Claims
- Scenario planning, scenario thinking and scenario analysis are interchangeable terms for what-if explorations of possible or preferable futures without aiming to quantify the likelihood of these situations; unlike falsifiable forecasts, scenarios resist systematic evaluation. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Backcasting is a normative foresight approach (introduced by John Robinson 1990) that looks back from a preferable future to today; because fantasizing about positive futures without a clear path correlates negatively with effort and success, visioning should be followed by a step-by-step backcast of necessary and contributing factors (per Oettingen and Mayer 2002). ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Role play is useful when key outcomes depend on the interacting decisions of multiple agents, when decision choice sets are ambiguous or poorly known, and when large numbers of complex organizations must work together (per Parson 1996). ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- Because of information hazards — the dissemination of true information that may cause harm (per Bostrom 2011) — participatory futures approaches are not particularly suitable for CBRN threat contexts; closed workshops with invited participants, non-disclosure agreements, and responsible vulnerability disclosure are more advisable than open participatory formats. ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])
- The US government's most realistic nuclear wargame of the Cold War, Proud Prophet (June 1983), ended in the massive destruction of the US, Europe and the Soviet Union with about one billion deaths, and may have contributed to a reduction in nuclear threat rhetoric by the Reagan administration in subsequent years (per Bracken 2012). ([[Raw/kohlerStrategicForesightKnowledge2021]])

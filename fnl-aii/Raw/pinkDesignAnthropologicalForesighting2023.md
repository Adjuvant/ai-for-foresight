    1.  Introduction
                  In this article we introduce and demonstrate design anthropological foresighting as an approach to understanding and reframing how
    people will live with emerging and automated technologies in possible futures (in the example discussed here, 2030 and 2050). Design
    anthropological foresighting investigates the experiential and processual dimensions of emergent futures, as well as the values, pri-
    orities, relationships and social practices that shape how futures come about. Importantly, in studies of automation and futures, it shifts
    attention from the idea that human and environmental futures will be shaped by automated technologies. Instead, it suggests how, in
    near and far futures, people will use automated features of technologies to continuously reconstitute and monitor everyday life (Pink
    et al., 2023).
                         Automated and connected systems and technologies are increasingly integrated in everyday life, both visibly and apparent to
    people, and as they invisibly inhabit the mundane technologies, tasks and processes that form the infrastructures of the everyday
    (Algorithm Watch, 2020; Burgess et al., 2022; Pink et al., 2022). Moreover, the technology industry, business consultancies, gov-
    ernment and policy makers create and ascribe to promissory narratives concerning the supposed societal problems that emerging
    technologies driven by artificial intelligence (AI) and Automated Decision-Making (ADM) will solve, and the subsequent societal
    benefits they are predicted to bring (Morozov, 2013; Jasanoff, 2015). For example, smart home systems have promised to deliver
    seamless home-based internet-of-things                      (IoT) systems interconnecting smart devices and making home life more convenient and

to understand and ultimately to qualitatively forecast changing Australian digital lifestyle trends and their impact on future household
electricity demand. Here, we centre work originally reported to an industry audience in our Digital Energy Futures: Foresights for Future
Living report (Pink, Korsmeyer et al., 2022) which presented a set of foresights and re-framing concepts, and subsequently informed a
set of design ethnographic futures scenarios (Strengers et al., 2023).
    The ambition of this article is methodological, rather than an attempt to define or predict futures. Ethnographic foresighting is not a
predictive method. It identifies how people and things are likely to be configured in futures rather than exactly what people will do or
directly experience in the future. In this article          we use the term design ethnographic foresighting when referring to foresighting
methods that involve ethnographic futures research, while we use anthropological foresighting when discussing specifically uses of
such methods when they are shaped by design anthropological theory. In what follows we first establish the context relating to how
automated or technology futures are constituted and presented in dominant narratives and the roles they play in predicting futures. We
then outline the background to design ethnographic foresighting and discuss its relationship to futures and design anthropological
theory and social practice theory. Next we discuss the development and delivery of the stages that contribute to design ethnographic
foresighting: futures-focused ethnography, surveys, ethnographic documentary making, and design ethnographic futures workshops.
We demonstrate the layers of knowledge these different approaches offer. We then show how this knowledge can be used to produce
two outcomes: specific foresights relating to everyday futures; and reframing concepts which can be used to critically complicate
existing future visions. To conclude we outline the wider applications of design anthropological foresighting and encourage colleagues
to engage in the theoretical and methodological debate and advancement it calls for.
2.  Theorising possible technology futures
    A significant literature across media studies  and political science reports on the "technological solutionism",        "sociotechnical
imaginaries", power relations, and the possible consequences for society of dominant industry and government stakeholder technology
future visions (e.g. Morozov, 2013, Jasanoff, 2015). Their (rightly) critical views of the promissory narratives in which technological
solutions will deliver multiple societal benefits emphasise the inequalities which underpin and are exacerbated by dominant tech-
nology visions.                However, while we must be aware of the dystopian consequences implied by their logics, other work shows that
attention to the everyday realities where people shape how automated technologies and systems come about suggests rather different
outcomes (T. Markham, 2020, Pink et al., 2022, Pink, 2023). The agenda of design anthropological foresighting involves going beyond
simple critique or binary contestation of dominant technology future visions, and quantitative assumptions with qualitative findings.
Instead we wish to develop a pre-emptive approach which surfaces realistic and plausible futures, to reveal how the flawed logics of
technological solutionism will not play out as intended, and instead suggests how assumptions about technology futures might be
reframed. While there is a long interest in futures in anthropology (see Collins, 2021), theoretical misalignment between earlier an-
thropologies of the future, and waves of anthropological theory led to a tardy incorporation of futures into the discipline's mainstream
(Pink  &              Salazar, 2017). Recently, two strands have emerged, and we argue that now is the time to build on these to generate a
movement in design anthropological foresight.
           The Futures Anthropology Manifesto (in Salazar et al., 2017) engages anthropologists critically and interventionally in possible
futures. Futures anthropology is undertaken in and with futures, investigates the unknowable and the possible and departs from the
discipline's traditional recourse to situating its knowledge in the past. Design anthropology has been characterised as a reframing
approach in existing literatures (see Kjaersgaard  & Otto, 2012, Otto  &        Smith, 2013). Design and futures anthropology involve spec-
ulative  and     unsettling modes of intervention  and in our  design  anthropology practice are mobilised through   the        theoretical

commitments of phenomenological anthropology (eg Ingold, 2000, 2010, 2021). Design anthropologists emphasise: the indeterminacy
of futures, and theories of emergence, uncertainty and possibility; the contingency and improvisation of everyday life circumstances
and actions; participatory and collaborative practice (Smith & Otto, 2016; Irving, 2017; Akama et al., 2018); the mobilisation of
future-oriented concepts such as hope and trust; and the question of what the future might and should "feel like" (Pink, 2023).
   The anthropology of the future (Bryant & Knight, 2019), in contrast, aligns with the comparative agenda of traditional anthro-
pology, and engages social practice theory (SPT) to understand how anticipation is manifested in everyday practices. It aligns with our
concept of a "social practice imaginary" (Strengers et al., 2019) which can be mobilised to propose speculative future scenarios, for
example where we have asked questions of how future "stay-at-home pets" will be implicated in future social practices. As developed in
sociology (e.g. Shove et al., 2012) SPT has been used successfully in studies of historical changes and to inform interventions in the
present, by showing how practices shift over time and co-evolve in relation to new technologies, changing conventions and cultural
understandings, and new forms of practical knowledge (Shove et al., 2012). Similarly, by tracking changing practices into the future
we consider how automated technologies and energy systems might transform and support future social practices in extreme weather
situations, or how they may change people's expectations for comfort, entertainment and the home itself.
   Our foresighting method therefore draws on these theoretical approaches, which in common, call for an emphasis on how futures
emerge in everyday life and the contingent and uncertain experience and practices this entails. It also draws on existing approaches to
qualitative and ethnographic foresighting, discussed next.
3. Background to design ethnographic foresighting
   In reviewing the extensive existing literature on foresighting, Saritas and colleagues' comment "The trend towards digitalization
implies that Foresight is at the threshold of a new era", will "will most likely bring human and machine intelligence together to
anticipate the future" and qualitative and quantitative methods are increasingly brought together into foresighting processes (Saritas
et al., 2022). Jari Kaivo-oja suggests that in "participatory foresight studies, where qualitative methods are often used in FTA [Futures
oriented technology analysis] studies" such "phenomenological research can enrich our understanding about the everyday life expe-
rience of people" and may transform foresight practices (Kaivo-oja, 2017: 95-6). Such approaches encourage attention to process, as
Stewart Allen points out, where much foresighting has been "driven by a static model or pre-defined categories", an approach to
"foresight as a process and unfolding sets of relations that can be mapped ethnographically" helping to "avoid the fallacy of demar-
cation and definition" (Allen, 2021 no page numbers). Allen sees an "ethnographic approach to futuring" as being located in the in-
terdependencies between foresight ("predicting what the future could look like") and planning (predicting "what the future should look
like according to a pre-defined schema") (Allen, 2021 no page numbers). For Stuart Candy and Kelly Kornet implementing this has
meant "turning foresight inside out" (Candy & Kornet 2019: 19), through their "Ethnographic Experiential Futures" (EXF) approach
which brings together the approaches of futurists, designers, and researchers in a structured process of: "Map, Multiply, Mediate,
Mount, and Map again" (Candy & Kornet 2019: 14). They advocate for extending "critical and participatory foresight work into a
deeply embodied mode, by scaffolding processes to more effectively explore the futures thinking of diverse communities, using design"
(Candy & Kornet 2019: 13). Notably, these approaches, developed predominantly in business-focused research, bring together the
qualitative and quantitative to foreground the processual and experiential dimensions of possible futures.
   "Ethnographic foresighting" has also been developed in HCI research to investigate how people are likely to experience possible
future situations. In this approach, the foresights themselves are differentiated from the ethnographic findings concerning what people
do when participating in hypothetical scenarios or situation, and do not predict what people will do in the future. Instead, foresights
report on what people's priorities and needs are likely to be as these situations play out and continue to evolve. That is, foresights do
not refer to static future situations or end points, but they seek to identify how people, things and environments will move forward into
uncertain futures. For example, Thomas Lindgren and colleagues (Lindgren, Pink & Fors, 2021) discuss the case of the self-driving car,
an emerging technology which has been poised to become part of our lives for over five years, but is still not ubiquitous. Most people
have no experience of AD cars, therefore, Lindgren and colleagues explain, in everyday life they "learn to live with AD technologies
through their evolving experiences of factors including ADAS [Advanced Driver Assistance Systems] in cars, media news, and com-
panies' AD visions" (Lindgren et al., 2021: 2). As these AD technologies themselves are not static, "people's anticipations of AD will also
ongoingly evolve, rather than having an endpoint in a specific state" (2021:3). Lindgren et al. argue that ethnographic foresighting offers
an approach through which to understand how people's engagements with hypothetical technologies come about in everyday life,
where there is no "before and after state" of people having "accepted" the technologies. Reviewing existing work on the relationship
between insights, forecasting and foresights, they establish two key points: "First, foresight is non-deterministic, in that it has a
multiple vision of futures and is open to opportunities rather than being predictive" and "Second, foresight encourages proactivity, and
the possibility of, and ability to, appropriate the future away from the forecasts that predetermine the path to the future" (2021: 4).
   The design and analysis of ethnographic research (and by extension ethnographic foresighting research) is always inflected by the
theoretical and conceptual orientations of academic disciplines (whether implicitly or explicitly). The studies reviewed in this section
emphasise the practical and methodological, rather than theoretical, dimensions of ethnographic foresighting. In the next section we
demonstrate how the layers of futures knowledge and foresight created in our Digital Energy Futures project were shaped theoretically
and methodologically.
4. Design anthropological foresighting in practice: digital energy futures
   In the Digital Energy Futures project, three theoretical approaches were engaged across the multi-staged research, each of which

a social practice theory design and analysis of seven "practice domains" of everyday life activity in the present and in possible futures;
and a phenomenological anthropology design and analysis of the experiential dimensions of possible futures. .
   An industry report analysis (stage 1) was undertaken of 64 technology and energy industry reports published between 2010 and
2020, with the majority published in 2018 (18) and 2019 (32). The review included both Australian and international reports but
focused on those trends which were likely to impact Australian households. The analysis gathered visions, scenarios and predictions
explicitly proposed in the reports, and through a qualitative content analysis, identified 14 macrotrends including large-scale tech-
nology, environmental, demographic or economic trends and disruptions expected to affect near (2025-2030) and far (2030-2050)
futures (Dahlgren et al., 2020). While the report analysis allowed us to establish a baseline of industry visions, such an analysis of
socio-technical imaginaries is limited in its capacity to account for the real circumstances of everyday life experience and practice, and
how this does and is likely to shape the ways in which emerging technologies come about and become part of future societal con-
figurations (Strengers et al., 2022; Dahlgren et al., 2021). To counter this, we drew on a set of established anthropological and so-
ciological principles, rooted in existing empirical research, which enabled us to not only identify the weaknesses in the sociotechnical
imaginaries analysed, but to also see beyond the logics of critique promoted by scholars of sociotechnical imaginaries who seek to
identify what could go wrong if dominant sociotechnical imaginaries play out, rather than how and why they are unlikely to play out as
envisioned. Where techno-solutionist narratives outlined in the reports suggested that everyday life activities would be shaped by new
technologies, we drew on the large body of research in digital anthropology that has demonstrated that people improvise to shape and
adapt technologies to the demands of everyday circumstances (see Horst & Miller, 2012), and demonstrate that these industry nar-
ratives are implausible. For instance, a reviewed report from Australia's national science agency, CSIRO, claims "Artificial Intelligence
(AI) is one of those technologies, like advances in gene editing or quantum computing, which has the power to change life itself"
(CSIRO 2019a quoted in Dahlgren et al., 2020, 42). Such top-down and technology directed vision fails to account for not only the
false-starts of such technologies (Dafoe, 2015) but also assumes that social life will be unidirectionally changed by technology, rather
than how such technology will also be transformed by people's adaptations, improvisations, and tailoring of these technologies as they
incorporate them into their everyday lives. The industry report analysis was also used to create innovative research materials in the

   Ethnographic futures research (stage 2) was undertaken with 72 households in Victoria and New South Wales in Australia, to
include: early adopters of digital technologies or energy technologies; new estate homes; apartment renters; high energy demand
homes; sea or tree changers; and rural agricultural areas. 23 were culturally or linguistically diverse. The households included people
living alone, nuclear and intergenerational families, and accounted for diversity across gender, age and incomes. Our research engaged
social practice theory to structure our field sites and analysis of the present and future into seven "practice domains". These domains
captured current and emerging everyday practices of significance to our research partners and residential energy demand. Ethnog-
raphy invites us to immerse ourselves in other people's lives and futures ethnography requires creative methods for invoking possible
(rather than predicted) experiential futures which researchers can share with participants and thus introduce or invite them to imagine
future worlds or scenarios, which they would not have encountered or considered had they not participated in the research. Our team
achieved this with the 72 participating households, employing established "short term ethnography" techniques (Pink & Morgan,
2013) to create intensive encounters with people's lives in their homes, through interview, video tours, participant-produced materials
and participant's engagements with the future-focused comic strips. We met participants through video calls necessitated during the
pandemic and in-person during our pilot and documentary filmmaking process. Our findings were presented in two ways: in our Future
Home Life report, organised according to the social practice domains that we analysed (Strengers et al., 2021); and in the Digital Energy
Futures (Pink, 2022) ethnographic documentary film, based on home visits to explore people's experiences, values and future visions
for EVs, battery charging, energy, automation and control.
   To differentiate between research findings and foresight, Lindgren and colleagues (2021) identified the need for people to be able to
choose when to let automation happen, and when to make their own decisions. They call this "being in the moment" in terms of
research findings. As a foresight however, this translates as the need for people to stay "in control". We likewise used the findings from
this stage of the research to generate foresights. To do so we brought them into dialogue with survey data and design ethnographic
futures workshop findings, which we outline next.
   The Energy Consumers Behavioural Survey (ECBS) (stage 3) is run annually by our partner organisation, Energy Consumers
Australia. Our team contributed a set of "digital energy futures" questions, derived from our ethnographic findings, to the survey. We
aimed to test and quantify the trends we had identified in our ethnographic fieldwork, to better understand their broader significance
for the Australian population, and to help us focus on areas for developing foresights. The survey allowed us to validate our insights,
understand how widespread and prevalent they are, and track them over time, as the survey is longitudinal, having been run so far in
2021 and 2022. The survey was included in the research design to give confidence to the ethnographic research and foresights, given
the energy industry has a long history of prioritising and valuing large-scale customer data over smaller qualitative trials and research
projects (Strengers et al., 2019). Knowing this, the survey data were used in our analysis and presentation of research to cross-check
our findings, and to confirm (or moderate) our ethnographic findings. Through the ECBS we accounted for 4661 national survey
responses over two years.
   Design ethnographic futures workshops (stage 4) built on our existing findings to respond to a set of key questions: regarding "How
people are likely to make decisions about energy and technology in the future, on the basis of their own knowledge and expertise";
"How people are likely to want to feel (emotionally and sensorially) in the future, and how they will use energy and technology to
ensure this"; "How people are likely to change their everyday life priorities and routines in future circumstances where climate,
technology, and society is changing"; "What values are likely to become important to people in the future"; "The significance of place
and locality for how people will live in the future" (Pink et al., 2022b). We delivered 10 workshops with a total of 42 people, 50% each
men and women, with diversity across age, homeowners and renters, people who had and had not experienced energy hardship and
those who had new energy technologies.
   To respond to our questions, we selected three areas to explore in depth, based on our ethnographic findings and the priorities of
our energy sector research partners in relation to technological changes which could increase or lead to shifts in energy demand. Thus
the workshops were shaped by a contextual focus on future localities, place and extreme weather, and empirical focus on: the everyday
futures of electric vehicles (EVs); future air technologies for heating, cooling, purification and filtration; and the intersection of future
daily routines. EVs and future home air technologies were treated as emerging automated technologies because they were not
ubiquitous in Australia. Most participants had experienced air technologies for heating and cooling and many had experienced or
heard of air filtration and purification technologies. They had also recently experienced disruptive changes to their air environments,
both outdoors and at home (due to the Covid-19 pandemic, bushfire smoke, allergies and Asthma thunderstorms), and could perceive
the possibility of future threats to their air safety and quality. Most participants were aware of existing possibilities relating to EVs,
some owned EVs or knew people who did. However, none had experienced living in an environment which was fully adapted to
accessible and inclusive EV charging, the emergence of other possible future EV services, and the possibility that features of these EV
technologies and services could be automated in the future.
   People can find it difficult to imagine futures beyond their existing worlds (A. Markham, 2021) and moreover may not have had
opportunities to engage with emerging technologies in real everyday life circumstances - often because the technologies have not
reached markets or become ubiquitous. Subsequently "this limits the capacity of both forecasting and user insight studies to realis-
tically and plausibly account for what people's future needs [of these technologies] … might be" (Lindgren et al., 2021: 1). Moreover, it
was impossible for us to inhabit versions of possible air quality/technology, EV or everyday routine futures with participants in any
sustained way. Any "ethnographies of the possible" - in which people encounter possible futures scenarios (Halse, 2013) that we
engaged in with them needed to be highly constructed, out of everyday time (ie in a workshop setting) and ethnographies needed to be
"short term" (Pink & Morgan, 2013) to accommodate our project schedule and scope. In response to this challenge we built on a

virus of the Covid 19 pandemic) in their local neighbourhoods, thus considering their futures in relation to real concerns for the
comfort, safety and wellbeing of themselves, their families and the environment, invoked by ways of knowing that were active in the
present. .
   The tensions that this technique created between how people know in the present, and how they could imagine the (always un-
certain) future were both productive and challenging. For our workshops it was vital that we engaged with participants through their
local knowledge of their environments and activities in the present, as a means of invoking possible futures. This meant that rather than
there being one route to exploring possible futures with participants, the techniques we used were designed differently for each
question. For example, in the EV session the primacy of local knowledge and ways of knowing led participants to reflect on what was
possible in the present as a basis for what might be needed in the future, bringing forth physical limitations and possible ways forward
in relation to these. Simultaneously while participants engaged with possible futures by invoking apps and digital payment systems, we
often needed to remind them of or propose to them alternative digital technologies and infrastructures that might participate in
possible futures. In contrast, in the air technology session participants drew on a wider range of their own experiences of using digital
voice assistants, smartphones, and self-tracking technologies, for example in exploring questions of digital privacy and control, while
also situating future experiences and practices locally, in this case in relation to local weather systems and local outdoor cooking and
other activities like fires and burning which impacted air quality.
   Therefore, each workshop activity enabled us to engage with different dimensions of what participants sensed and believed that
they and other people would experience, and enact within the parameters of specific possible future situations. As design ethnogra-
phers we inhabited these possible futures with them and their imagined future neighbours, seeking to listen, sense and visualise their

argument that, particularly post-pandemic "Foresight should be brought closer to the sites of its application". In bringing design
ethnographic foresighting into dialogue with our critical review of industry visions of energy and technology futures, we correspond
with Saritas et al.'s call for "Foresight-on-site", to "go beyond the anticipation of future developments … to consider the further
impacts of science, research, policies, and actions on society and humanity with ethical, legal, cultural, and value implications". Design
anthropological foresight additionally generalises further beyond the sites of application, to create "reframing" concepts which are
corrective of such dominant visions, which we outline next.
6. Reframing concepts
   The dominant concepts that currently guide energy sector planning were identified from the industry content analysis (Dahlgren
et al., 2021), and past research (eg. Strengers et al., 2019). They were reframed by the research team by drawing together ethno-
graphic, workshops and survey findings, and our theoretical scholarship, to create and evidence 14 alternative concepts. In this section
we introduce two of the 14 reframing concepts, as examples which demonstrate how theoretical, conceptual and empirical findings can
be combined to suggest new frames through which to understand automation and futures. The process of reframing follows four steps,
which are developed in dialogue with each other, than in a necessarily linear process. They involve: identifying industry concepts and
the assumptions that underpin them; critically reviewing the industry concepts through empirically endorsed existing anthropological
and sociological theory and concepts; using our ethnographic, workshop and quantitative evidence to establish realistic visions of
possible futures; and developing new plausible and empirically (reframing) concepts which are theoretically and empirically sup-
ported by our research evidence and deliver realistic frames through which to envision possible everyday futures.
   The "Set and Notify" reframing concept, exemplified in Fig. 4, was developed in response to the dominant industry concept of "Set
and Forget", which assumes that consumers wish to have the convenience of setting devices in such a way that delegates control to a
third party through an automated system. Our ethnographic and workshop research found that participants did not wish to relinquish
control to automated systems in such a way and that they wished to be notified so that they could make informed decisions relating to
the control of technologies. For instance, participants felt that in the future they would increasingly depend on clean air for the safety
and health of their families, and thus needed to be notified about possible dangers and control their future air technologies to respond.
To generate new thinking about how notifications would be mobilised, in our academic scholarship (Pink et al., 2023), we explored
how participants would mobilise their embodied, experiential and local knowledge in relation to digital data, to make informed de-
cisions regarding what actions to take. The social practice theory lens showed everyday practices involving air technologies changing,
or anticipated to change, in relation to these changing technologies and health and safety concerns. When reframing "Set and Forget"
to "Set and Notify" in our report, we were careful to point out that, while our research findings indicate that people desire notifications,
in possible futures they are unlikely to appear as smartphone screen notifications. Here, rather than seeking to create visions of specific
future situations, it is the framing that we seek to correct. .
   A second example of our mobilisation of reframing concepts is demonstrated through the concept of "plug and play", which is a
well-known marketing phrase commonly associated with smart technology. "Plug and play" is increasingly used in the energy sector
with reference to emerging energy technologies. The idea is similar to "set and forget" in the sense that people are expected to plug a
device into a power socket, and immediately access a seamless user experience that offers very little engagement or effort on their part,
enabling them to "play" with the settings and benefits. We reframed this concept to "Everyday Innovators", which draws attention to
the continually modification, adaptation and tailoring of devices into people's everyday environments and practices. Rather than
seeing people as users or adopters of new technology, the concept of Everyday Innovators reframes people as curators of technologies
for home environments, where devices are retrofitted and sometimes rejected to fit into people's everyday routines. Everyday In-
novators curate an ecosystem of devices within their homes to generate the most seamless experience they can, recognising that
continually tinkering is desired and required.
7. Foresighting for indeterminate and incomplete futures
   We argue that the qualitative social sciences - here anthropology and sociology - have the potential to play a key role in creating
new foresight for our possible futures. In this article we have proposed a design anthropological foresighting practice which seeks to
engage with indeterminate futures, by creating propositions about the basis upon which human experiences and practices will shape
possible futures. Such a practice contributes by shifting the capability of these disciplines to comprehend and categorise possible
futures and the processes through which they come about, and to encounter them empirically through new methods. To demonstrate
this we have outlined a new approach to design anthropological foresighting, exemplified through our Digital Energy Futures project.

We have shown how design anthropological and social practice theories can be mobilised for this purpose, and how speculative design
futures and "thing ethnography" methods can be applied.
   Theoretical scholarship plays a key role in design anthropological foresighting and we have demonstrated how we have also drawn
from our respective commitments to theories of sociotechnical imaginaries, design and futures anthropology and social practice. This
we propose is the differentiating feature of the design anthropological foresighting approach. To existing ethnographic foresighting it
adds the wealth of empirically endorsed theoretical and empirical research, while simultaneously opening this existing theoretical and
conceptual corpus to being advanced further in response to empirical work. The ethnographic-theoretical dialogue characteristic of
anthropological practice offers the opportunity not only to anticipate possible human futures, but to propose conceptual categories
through which to interpret and "frame" the ways people are likely to live in the future. Thus assisting us as a device for reframing
dominant understandings of the future of automation and society. In our experience the approaches of phenomenologically oriented
design and futures anthropology and social practice theory offered different but complementary lenses through which to understand
and represent dimensions of possible futures. In common these theories and the concepts they mobilise account for the processual, non-
representational (sensory and affective) and unspoken ways of knowing through which everyday life proceeds and shifts over time.
They are therefore particularly suitable in underpinning an approach to foresighting that acknowledges the relationships between the
uncertainty and contingency of possible futures and human action and experience. However, beyond (but building on) its relationship
to scholarship and critique, design anthropological foresighting is a generative practice. Foresights open "the problem space and could
drive implications for designing … that differ from the idealistic visions" (Lindgren et al., 2021: 13). Thus, design anthropological
foresights can offer alternative perspectives which both take the experiential, sensory and affective qualities of possible futures as a
starting point for future design and can enable researchers to generate "social practice imaginaries". Because design anthropological
foresighting involves a dialogue with critical and generative theories, it can be particularly useful when it is necessary to demonstrate
critiques of existing future visions, while simultaneously proposing realistic, optimistic and hopeful alternatives. While in this article
we have discussed this in relation to examples that focus on futures that concern people, energy and automated technology, design
anthropological foresighting is not limited to these questions, but can be employed to address a wide range of futures questions.
   In this article we have suggested an approach to anthropological foresighting, however it is important to be aware that the
ethnographic practice that underpins this is not based in a single or fixed method. Anthropological ethnography involves continuous
methodological innovation and its analysis is characterised by diverse and infrequently documented methods (Winthereik & Balles-
tero, 2021). It is specific to the sites where it is situated and in the questions it explores and ethnographic knowledge is, like foresight
(Allen, 2021) always incomplete whether it concerns the past or the future.While some existing approaches - such as that proposed by
Candy and Kornet (2019) noted earlier - offer step by step approaches to developing foresight, the approach to anthropological
foresighting we propose is more "messy" and uneven, it requires us to follow the "ethnographic hunches" (Pink, 2021) that emerge in
any one research process to create stories that make sense according to the theoretical and empirical logics available to us. Therefore,
in presenting and demonstrating our approach, rather than calling on other researchers to replicate the precise steps we have taken, we
invite colleagues to join us in furthering the possibilities of ethnographic-theoretical dialogue and for encountering possible futures
with participants in research and with research partners. We encourage further experimentation and theoretical and conceptual debate
concerning how to account for futures ethnographically through the social sciences.
